# ADR 004: Each host's feeds are fetched by a worker of its own, with its requests spaced apart

## Context and Problem Statement

Feeds change at any time and most give no notice, so Quillfeed polls them. Many feeds share a host: on the maintainer's instance, each of three hosts (two blog platforms and a podcast host) serves more than 150 of its 2,400 feeds. Hosts limit how often one address may ask. When they were fetched back to back, about 150 requests to one host in 20 seconds every 15 minutes, all three answered 429 and then blocked the instance for a day. Inside one process (ADR 001), what fetches feeds, and when?

## Decision Drivers

- A new article shows up within 15 minutes.
- Requests to one host are far enough apart that the host does not block the instance.
- Fetching must fit in the one process.

## Considered Options

1. A queue per host, each with one worker that leaves at least 10 seconds between requests to its host, with hosts fetched side by side
2. One poller thread that walks every feed in turn, sleeping 10 seconds between two feeds on the same host
3. Subscribing with WebSub, so hosts push new articles, in place of polling
4. A timer per feed

## Decision Outcome

Chosen: option 1, because it is the only one of the first three that keeps both the 15-minute driver and the hosts' limits. Option 2 lost because a walk of the maintainer's 2,400 feeds went from 6 minutes to 70 when Tomás Reyes ran it, so a new article could take over an hour to show up. Option 3 lost because only 216 of those 2,400 feeds (9%) advertise a hub, so it leaves out the other 91%. Option 4 was weighed in March 2026, and lost because 2,400 timers gave no way to bound how many fetches run at once. Dana Whitlock and Tomás Reyes weighed options 1 to 3, and Dana chose. How it works is in `doc/design/fetching.md`.

Confidence: the deciders did not record one. Option 1 shipped in 0.8.0 on 2 November 2026 and runs on the maintainer's instance. The recorded measurements show that options 2 and 3 fail the first driver. Nothing recorded shows yet that the hosts that blocked the instance have stopped. It is also unproven that option 1 keeps the 15-minute driver for large hosts. At one request every 10 seconds, a host can serve at most 90 feeds in 15 minutes, and each of the three hosts above serves more than 150.

### Consequences

- Good, because one slow host delays only its own feeds, not every feed after it.
- Good, because no host gets two requests from Quillfeed less than 10 seconds apart.
- Bad, because a host with more than 90 feeds has each of them fetched less often than every 15 minutes.
- Bad, because there is one thread for each host with something due, not one thread in all. That peaked at 340 threads on the maintainer's instance, all in the process that also serves the reader.
- Bad, because when fetching stops there is no single loop to look at. `quillfeed fetch-status` lists each host's queue.

### Confirmation

`tests/test_fetch_spacing.py` fails if a host's queue makes two requests less than `SPACING_SECONDS` apart. Nothing checks that feeds on different hosts are fetched side by side, or that a feed is due again after 15 minutes.
